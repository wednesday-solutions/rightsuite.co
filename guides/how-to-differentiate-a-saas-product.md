# How to Differentiate a SaaS Product When It Looks Like Your Competitors

You differentiate a SaaS product by picking one buyer you can be the obvious choice for, finding the reason that buyer would pick you over the alternatives they already know, and saying it in their words. Your feature list stays the same. What changes is who you're for and what you claim. Then test it against named competitors.

## Who this is for

Your product works, but your homepage could swap logos with two competitors and nobody would notice. Buyers ask "how are you different from X?" on every call, and you answer with a feature comparison. This is for early-stage B2B SaaS founders with a handful of customers or none, who need a sharper reason to be picked.

## Why do SaaS products end up looking the same?

Most products enter a category by studying what's there and building a cleaner version. That's a fine way to build. It's a bad way to position, because you inherit the category's copy along with its features: the same hero line, the same three benefit bullets, the same "simple, powerful, flexible".

Buyers compare what they can see. If what they see is the same, they fall back on price, brand and whoever they heard of first. You rarely win that comparison as the newer, smaller option.

## Step by step: how to differentiate your SaaS product

### Step 1: List the real alternatives

Write down what your buyer would do if you didn't exist. Include direct competitors, but also the spreadsheet, the agency, the intern and doing nothing. You're not competing with the whole category. You're competing with whatever this buyer would otherwise pick on the day they decide.

### Step 2: Choose one axis to lead with

There are four useful axes. Pick the one where you have the strongest true claim.

| Axis | Shape of the claim | Works when |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer | "Built for [specific type of team]" | One segment has needs the big tools ignore |
| Workflow | "Handles [process] end to end" | Competitors cover part of a job and leave the rest manual |
| Outcome | "Gets you [result] in [timeframe]" | You can show a faster or more certain result |
| Price and setup | "For teams that don't need [incumbent's] overhead" | The leader is built for bigger buyers than yours |

Trying to lead with all four is how you end up saying nothing. One axis, said clearly, beats four said vaguely.

### Step 3: Find the evidence you already have

If you have customers, look at why they chose you. Pull your won deals and early sign-ups and look for a common thread: same company type, same trigger, same competitor they left. That pattern is your strongest candidate because the market already voted for it.

If you have no customers yet, use your [customer discovery interviews]({{ '/guides/customer-discovery-interviews/' | relative_url }}). Ask people who fit your target what they use now, what annoys them about it, and what would make them switch. The answer that keeps coming up is your opening.

### Step 4: Write a positioning anchor

Turn the pattern into one sentence: "For [specific buyer] who [specific situation], [product] is the [category] that [specific outcome], unlike [named alternative] which [what it does worse for this buyer]." This is the anchor your homepage, sales calls and ads are written from. It isn't your tagline. [How to write a positioning statement]({{ '/guides/positioning-statement-for-saas/' | relative_url }}) has more worked versions.

### Step 5: Narrow until it feels slightly uncomfortable

The urge is to keep it broad so you don't exclude anyone. Resist it. A buyer who reads "built for seed-stage B2B SaaS teams running a free trial" knows instantly whether it's them. A buyer who reads "built for growing teams" has to work it out, and most won't bother. Start with the narrowest version that's still a real market and expand once you own it.

### Step 6: Test it against named competitors

Show your anchor and your homepage next to your top alternatives and ask buyers which they'd pick and why. Listen for whether they repeat your claim back. If they describe you in the category's generic words, the differentiation isn't landing yet.

## Symptom, check, fix

| Symptom | Check | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Buyers ask "how are you different from X?" | Does your hero line name who you're for? | Lead with the buyer or workflow axis, not features |
| You win only on price | Is there a reason beyond cost in your copy? | Tie price to a specific buyer who doesn't need the incumbent |
| Buyers put you in the wrong category | What do they call you when describing you? | State the category plainly before the differentiator |
| Prospects like you but pick the leader | Is your claim believable without proof? | Add one specific proof point: a demo, an example, a guarantee |
| Your copy could fit a competitor's site | Swap logos. Does anything break? | Rewrite around the anchor sentence from step 4 |

If your page and a rival's are nearly interchangeable, [what to do when your SaaS looks exactly like the competition]({{ '/guides/saas-looks-like-competitors/' | relative_url }}) goes deeper on the copy side.

## A worked example

For example, a founder sells a scheduling tool for clinics. Their homepage says "smart scheduling for modern teams", the same message as two bigger rivals. Looking at their first 12 customers, nine are physiotherapy clinics with two to five practitioners, and several say they left a bigger tool because it was built for hospitals and took weeks to set up.

The new anchor: "For small physio clinics that need online booking live this week, [product] is the scheduling tool set up in an afternoon, unlike [big rival], which is built for multi-site hospitals." Same product. A different buyer would now recognise themselves in the first line, and the big rival's size becomes the reason to pick the small one. The numbers are illustrative; the method is the part to copy.
