# SaaS Price Increases: When to Raise, How Much, and How to Test It First

Raise your SaaS price when buyers stop pushing back on it and you've shipped real value since you set it. Size the increase from your own evidence, test the new number with simulated buyers in [RightPrice](https://rightsuite.co/products/right-price?utm_source=docs&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=docs&utm_content=guide-when-to-raise-saas-prices), roll it out to new customers first, and only then decide about existing ones.

## What is the average SaaS price increase?

Software prices are rising across the market. [Vertice](https://www.vertice.one/insights/saas-inflation-rate), which tracks software spend for buyers, put SaaS inflation at 14.3% in its preliminary September 2026 data, and says vendors keep making double-digit price increases.

That tells you two things. Your buyers are used to price increases from their other tools, and their finance teams are watching software spend closely. It doesn't tell you what your increase should be. A product that has doubled in capability can justify more than the market average; one that hasn't changed since last year will struggle to justify any.

## When should you raise your SaaS prices?

Look for signals that buyers are getting more value than they pay for. One on its own is noise. Two or three together are a decision.

1. **Price objections have gone quiet.** Read your last 10 to 20 lost deals and sales calls. If price is rarely the reason, you have room.
2. **Deals close faster or more often than your baseline.** Compare close rate and time-to-close for the last quarter against the one before. Demand rising at the same price is a pricing signal.
3. **Customers call you cheap.** "Great value," "surprisingly affordable" and "I'd pay more for this" in calls, reviews or support tickets mean exactly what they say.
4. **You've shipped real value since the last price.** List what's been added since the price was set. If a customer would describe the product as a different tool now, the price is out of date.
5. **Your costs went up.** If AI inference, data or infrastructure costs have risen per customer, an increase protects your margin.

Don't raise when churn is climbing, when onboarding is broken, or in the middle of a big product change. Fix those first, because a price increase makes every existing problem louder.

## How much should you raise your price?

Size it from evidence, not a round number.

1. **List what's changed.** Features shipped, outcomes customers now get, support added. This is also your announcement copy.
2. **Check close alternatives.** Note what the two or three products your buyers compare you to charge for a similar package. You don't need to match them, but you need to know where you sit.
3. **Pick two or three candidates.** For example, if you charge $49/month, test $59, $69 and $79.
4. **Test each candidate** with simulated buyers (below), then with real prospects.
5. **Choose the highest price that adds no new objections.** If $69 brings out objections that $59 didn't, $59 is your number for now.

## How to roll out a price increase

1. **New customers first.** Change the pricing page and new quotes. Existing customers stay where they are.
2. **Set the success test before you start.** Write down your current close rate, trial-to-paid rate and average deal size, and the drop you'd treat as "too far."
3. **Measure for a full sales cycle.** Compare new numbers against your baseline. Flat close rate at a higher price means the market accepts it.
4. **Then decide on existing customers.** Options: keep them at the old price (grandfathering), raise them by less than new customers, or raise them later with notice. Whatever you pick, tell them in plain words what they've gained, when the change happens, and what they can do (like lock in an annual plan at today's rate).
5. **Give notice.** For existing customers, send the notice well ahead of the change and check your contracts for any required notice period.

## Worked example

For example, a founder charges $49/month for a scheduling tool and hasn't changed it in 18 months. Since then, the product added team calendars and payment collection. In the last 15 lost deals, price came up twice. Two customers called it "a steal" in onboarding calls.

The founder tests $59, $69 and $79 in RightPrice. At $79, the skeptic persona and the competitor-minded persona both say a known alternative covers the same ground for less. At $69, objections focus on wanting annual billing, not on the price itself. The founder launches $69 for new customers with an annual option, keeps existing customers at $49 for six months, and tracks close rate against the previous quarter.

## Symptom, check, fix

| Symptom | Check | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Close rate dropped after the increase | Did objections change, or did lead quality change? | Compare lost-deal reasons before and after; step back to the lower candidate if price is the new reason. |
| Existing customers angry | Did the announcement explain what they've gained? | Lead with what changed, offer an annual lock-in, give more notice. |
| Everyone moves to the cheapest plan | Did you raise all tiers by the same amount? | Raise the tier with the most value added; keep an entry point. |
| No change in revenue | Is the new price only on new signups with few signups? | Give it a full sales cycle before judging. |
